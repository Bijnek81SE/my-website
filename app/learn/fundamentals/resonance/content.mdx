<LessonSection id="meaning" title="What resonance means">
  Resonance does not mean that a molecule rapidly switches between different
  structures. The molecule has one real electronic structure, called the
  **resonance hybrid**. The contributors are bookkeeping models that help us
  represent delocalised electrons.

  <div className="mt-6">
    <Callout title="Key idea" tone="success">
      Atoms remain in the same positions. Only electrons move when one resonance
      contributor is converted into another.
    </Callout>
  </div>
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="rules" title="Rules for drawing resonance contributors">
  1. Keep the same atom connectivity in every contributor.
  2. Move only π electrons or lone-pair electrons.
  3. Do not move atoms or σ bonds.
  4. Preserve the overall charge of the species.
  5. Use valid Lewis structures with appropriate valence shells.
  6. Separate contributors with a double-headed resonance arrow.
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="arrows" title="Curved-arrow notation">
  A curved arrow shows the movement of an electron pair. The arrow must begin at
  the electrons being moved and end where those electrons will be placed.

  <div className="mt-8">
    <CurvedArrowGuide />
  </div>

  <div className="mt-6 grid gap-4 sm:grid-cols-2">
    <Card title="Lone pair to bond">
      Start the arrow at a lone pair and point it toward an adjacent bond.
    </Card>
    <Card title="Bond to atom">
      Start the arrow at a π bond and point it toward the atom that will receive
      the electron pair.
    </Card>
  </div>
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="contributors" title="Major and minor contributors">
  Resonance contributors do not always contribute equally to the hybrid. More
  stable contributors make a larger contribution.

  <div className="mt-6 overflow-x-auto rounded-xl border border-slate-200">

  | More important | Less important |
  | --- | --- |
  | Complete octets | Incomplete octets |
  | Fewer formal charges | More charge separation |
  | Negative charge on electronegative atoms | Negative charge on less electronegative atoms |
  | Equivalent contributors contribute equally | Non-equivalent contributors may contribute unequally |

  </div>
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="examples" title="Worked examples">
  <div className="space-y-6">
    <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-6">
      <h3 className="text-xl font-semibold text-slate-900">Carboxylate ion</h3>
      <div className="mt-3 [&>p]:mt-0">
        A carboxylate ion has two equivalent contributors. In each one, one
        oxygen is double-bonded and the other bears the negative charge. Because
        the contributors are equivalent, the two carbon–oxygen bonds are
        identical in the resonance hybrid.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-6">
      <h3 className="text-xl font-semibold text-slate-900">Allylic cation</h3>
      <div className="mt-3 [&>p]:mt-0">
        In an allylic cation, the π bond can shift toward the positively charged
        carbon. The positive charge is therefore delocalised over two carbon
        atoms.
      </div>
    </div>

    <div className="rounded-xl border border-slate-200 bg-white p-6">
      <h3 className="text-xl font-semibold text-slate-900">Amide group</h3>
      <div className="mt-3 [&>p]:mt-0">
        The nitrogen lone pair can donate into the neighbouring carbonyl group.
        This gives the carbon–nitrogen bond partial double-bond character and
        helps explain the planarity of amides.
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="hybrid" title="The resonance hybrid">
  The resonance hybrid combines features of all significant contributors.
  Delocalised bonds are often drawn with dashed lines, and distributed charge
  may be shown with partial-charge symbols.

  <div className="mt-6">
    <Callout title="Consequences of delocalisation" tone="violet">
      - Bond lengths may become intermediate between single and double bonds.
      - Charge may be spread over several atoms.
      - The molecule or ion is often more stable than any one contributor
        suggests.
    </Callout>
  </div>
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="mistakes" title="Common mistakes">
  <div className="space-y-5">
    <div className="rounded-xl border border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-5">
      <h3 className="font-semibold text-slate-900">Moving atoms</h3>
      <div className="mt-2 [&>p]:mt-0">
        Resonance contributors must have the same atom connectivity. Moving a
        hydrogen or changing the molecular skeleton creates a different species,
        not a resonance contributor.
      </div>
    </div>
    <div className="rounded-xl border border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-5">
      <h3 className="font-semibold text-slate-900">Moving σ bonds</h3>
      <div className="mt-2 [&>p]:mt-0">
        Resonance arrows move lone pairs and π electrons. Breaking a σ bond
        usually represents a reaction step instead.
      </div>
    </div>
    <div className="rounded-xl border border-amber-200 bg-amber-50 p-5">
      <h3 className="font-semibold text-slate-900">Starting arrows at charges</h3>
      <div className="mt-2 [&>p]:mt-0">
        A curved arrow starts at electrons, not at a positive or negative charge
        symbol.
      </div>
    </div>
  </div>
</LessonSection>

<LessonSection id="summary" title="Summary">
  <SummaryBox>
    Resonance contributors are alternative Lewis structures with the same atom
    connectivity but different electron placement. Curved arrows show
    electron-pair movement, and the real molecule is a resonance hybrid in which
    electrons and charge may be delocalised.
  </SummaryBox>
</LessonSection>
